Chamomile

Matricaria chamomilla · Asteraceae (daisy family) · Calm & Mood, Restful Sleep, Digestion, Skin & Hair

Benefits at a glance

Chamomile is one of the gentlest herbs for calming the nerves and preparing the body for restful sleep. Its flavonoid apigenin acts on the brain's GABA receptors, which helps explain why small studies found better sleep quality and less anxiety. It relaxes the muscles of the digestive tract, easing gas, cramping and indigestion after meals. Its anti-inflammatory oils also soothe irritated skin, tired eyes and mild rashes when used as a compress. Mild enough for daily use, it is a comforting first herb for stress, sleep and digestion.

Overview

German chamomile has been brewed in Europe since ancient Egypt and Rome, and remains one of the most widely drunk herbal teas in the world. Its small white-and-gold flowers contain apigenin and the blue oil chamazulene, compounds researchers have studied for their calming and soothing qualities.

What it does in your body

  • Nervous system: Gently calms and eases the body toward sleep.
  • Digestive system: Relaxes cramping gut muscles and eases gas.
  • Skin: Reduces redness and irritation.

How it works chemically

  • Apigenin (Flavonoid): Binds to the benzodiazepine site on GABA-A receptors in the brain, mildly increasing the calming effect of GABA.
  • α-Bisabolol (Sesquiterpene alcohol): Lowers inflammatory prostaglandins and helps protect the stomach lining; also helps other compounds pass into the skin.
  • Chamazulene (Sesquiterpene (forms when steamed)): Blocks the formation of leukotrienes, inflammatory messengers, and acts as an antioxidant.

Benefits explained

Calm and better sleep (Some research)

Chamomile contains apigenin, a flavonoid that binds to the same calming GABA receptors in the brain targeted by some anti-anxiety medicines, giving a mild relaxing effect. Small clinical trials found chamomile extract improved sleep quality in older adults and eased symptoms of generalized anxiety.

Digestive comfort (Some research)

Compounds in chamomile relax the smooth muscle of the gut, which helps explain why it eases cramping, gas and that uncomfortable 'too full' feeling. It is a key ingredient in several European digestive remedies.

Soothing irritated skin (Traditional use)

Bisabolol and chamazulene — the oils that turn chamomile oil blue — calm inflammation. Cooled chamomile compresses and creams have long been used for mild rashes, sunburn and tired eyes.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 220–500 mg (Chamomile extract), 1–3 times a day.

The herb by itself: 2–3 g (Dried flowers steeped as tea (per cup)), 3–4 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • A cup in the evening to wind down before sleep
  • Settling an upset stomach, gas or mild cramping after meals
  • Cooled tea bags or rinses for tired eyes and irritated skin

How to prepare

Steep 1 heaped teaspoon of dried flowers (or 1 tea bag) in a covered cup of just-boiled water for 5–10 minutes. Covering the cup keeps the aromatic oils in.

Safety & cautions

Avoid if you are allergic to ragweed, daisies or marigolds. It may add to the effect of sedatives and can interact with blood thinners such as warfarin.
