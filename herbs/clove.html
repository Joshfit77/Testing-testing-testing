Clove

Syzygium aromaticum · Myrtaceae (myrtle family) · Culinary, Digestion, Aches & Joints

Benefits at a glance

Clove is a powerful spice best known for easing tooth pain. Its eugenol is a natural anesthetic and antiseptic that numbed gums as well as benzocaine in a dental study. Cloves rank among the highest of all foods in antioxidants. They warm the digestion and ease gas and nausea. A few cloves bring flavor and comfort to tea, chai and winter cooking.

Overview

Cloves are the unopened flower buds of an evergreen tree native to Indonesia's Spice Islands. They are rich in eugenol, which gives a numbing, warming effect — dentists once used clove oil for tooth pain, and eugenol is still found in some dental products.

What it does in your body

  • Mouth & teeth: Numbs tooth and gum pain; antiseptic.
  • Digestive system: Eases gas and nausea.
  • Whole body: Extremely high in antioxidants.

How it works chemically

  • Eugenol (Phenylpropanoid): Blocks sodium channels in nerves (a local anesthetic effect), inhibits COX enzymes, and kills bacteria.
  • β-Caryophyllene (Sesquiterpene): Activates CB2 receptors that calm inflammation without affecting the mind.
  • Tannins (Polyphenols): Astringent and antimicrobial.

Benefits explained

Tooth and gum pain (Well researched)

Eugenol is a natural anesthetic and antiseptic. In a dental study, a clove gel numbed the gums about as well as benzocaine gel.

Antioxidant powerhouse (Some research)

Ground cloves rank among the highest of all foods for antioxidant capacity.

Digestion (Traditional use)

Clove is used in Ayurvedic and Chinese medicine to ease gas, nausea and hiccups.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Not recommended as capsules — use whole cloves in tea or food.

The herb by itself: 0.1–0.3 g (2–4 whole cloves in tea or cooking), 1–3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • A traditional remedy for temporary toothache relief
  • Warming digestion in winter drinks
  • Spicing chai, mulled cider, ham and baked goods

How to prepare

Use whole cloves in simmered drinks and stews (remove before serving) or a small pinch of ground clove in baking.

Safety & cautions

Clove oil is very strong: do not swallow it, keep it away from children, and dilute it for skin. Eugenol may slow blood clotting.
