Dill

Anethum graveolens · Apiaceae (carrot family) · Digestion, Culinary

Benefits at a glance

Dill is a gentle digestive herb used for generations to ease gas and colic. Its oils relax the digestive tract and calm an upset stomach. Dill oil fights bacteria in laboratory studies, one reason it is used in pickling. The seeds provide calcium and the fronds add vitamins A and C. It is a soothing, flavorful herb for everyday cooking.

Overview

The name may come from the Old Norse dilla, 'to lull,' because dill water was given to soothe colicky babies. Both the delicate leaves and the seeds are used, especially in Scandinavian and Eastern European cooking.

What it does in your body

  • Digestive system: Relieves gas and colic.
  • Immune system: Mild antimicrobial effect.
  • Bones: Seeds provide calcium.

How it works chemically

  • Carvone (Monoterpene ketone): Relaxes smooth muscle in the digestive tract.
  • Limonene (Monoterpene): Supports digestion and is mildly antimicrobial.
  • Dillapiole (Phenylpropanoid): Antimicrobial and antispasmodic.

Benefits explained

Gas and colic (Traditional use)

Dill seed oil (carvone and limonene) relaxes the digestive tract. 'Gripe water' made with dill has been given for colic for centuries.

Fights germs (Some research)

Dill oil shows antibacterial activity in laboratory studies, one reason it was traditionally used in pickling.

Minerals and vitamins (Traditional use)

Dill seed contains calcium and other minerals, and fresh fronds add vitamins A and C.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Not commonly sold as capsules — use the seed as tea.

The herb by itself: 1–3 g (Crushed seed steeped as tea (per cup)), Up to 3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Dill seed tea after meals to ease gas
  • Flavoring pickles, potatoes, eggs, yogurt and salmon
  • A traditional, gentle remedy for hiccups

How to prepare

Add fresh fronds at the end of cooking. Steep 1 teaspoon crushed seed in hot water for 10 minutes for tea.

Safety & cautions

Safe as food. Some people develop skin sensitivity to sunlight after handling large amounts of the fresh plant.
