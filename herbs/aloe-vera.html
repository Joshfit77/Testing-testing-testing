Aloe Vera

Aloe barbadensis miller · Asphodelaceae · Skin & Hair

Benefits at a glance

Aloe vera is a cooling plant famous for soothing burns and sunburn. Studies found aloe gel helped burns heal faster than some standard dressings. Its polysaccharides hydrate the skin and calm redness and irritation. Aloe gels and rinses reduced plaque and gum inflammation in small studies. It is meant for use on the skin, not for eating.

Overview

Cleopatra is said to have used aloe in her beauty routine. The clear inner gel of its thick leaves is cooling and moisturizing, and is widely used for minor burns and sunburn. The yellow latex just under the skin is a strong laxative and is not the same as the gel.

What it does in your body

  • Skin: Cools and may help soothe minor burns and sunburn.
  • Skin: Hydrates and calms irritation.
  • Gums: Reduces gum inflammation (gels and rinses).

How it works chemically

  • Acemannan (Polysaccharide): Hydrates, encourages skin cells to multiply, and supports wound healing.
  • Glycoproteins & salicylic acid (Proteins / phenolic acid): Reduce inflammation and pain.
  • Aloin (in the yellow latex) (Anthraquinone glycoside): A strong laxative that irritates the bowel — the reason to drain the latex and avoid eating aloe.

Benefits explained

Burns and sunburn (Some research)

Studies found aloe gel helped first- and second-degree burns heal faster than some conventional dressings.

Skin hydration (Some research)

Aloe's polysaccharides draw moisture into the skin and calm irritation.

Gum health (Some research)

Aloe mouthwash and gels reduced plaque and gum inflammation in small studies.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Not recommended by mouth — aloe is for use on the skin.

For use on the skin only — do not swallow.

Traditional uses

  • Cooling relief for sunburn and minor kitchen burns
  • Moisturizing skin and hair
  • Soothing razor burn and skin irritation

How to prepare

Cut a leaf, let the yellow sap drain out for 10 minutes, then scoop out the clear gel. Store leftovers in the fridge.

Safety & cautions

For external use; patch test first. Do not eat aloe latex — it's a powerful laxative and is unsafe in large or regular amounts, especially during pregnancy.
