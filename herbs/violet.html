Sweet Violet

Viola odorata · Violaceae (violet family) · Skin & Hair, Breathing & Throat, Culinary

Benefits at a glance

Sweet violet is a fragrant flower that soothes the throat and skin. Its mucilage calms dry coughs, and violet syrup is a traditional cough remedy. Violet leaf salves soothe dry and irritated skin. The flowers contain the antioxidant rutin, which supports small blood vessels. The flowers are edible and beautiful on cakes and salads.

Overview

Sweet violets were a favorite of Napoleon and Josephine and were made into candies, syrups and perfumes. The flowers and leaves are edible and traditionally used for coughs, dry skin and swollen glands.

What it does in your body

  • Throat: Soothes dry coughs.
  • Skin: Soothes dryness.
  • Whole body: Antioxidant.

How it works chemically

  • Mucilage (Polysaccharides): Coats and soothes the throat.
  • Saponins (Glycosides): Help loosen mucus.
  • Rutin (Flavonoid): Antioxidant and supports small blood vessels.

Benefits explained

Dry coughs (Traditional use)

Violet's mucilage soothes the throat; violet syrup is a traditional cough remedy.

Soothing skin (Traditional use)

Violet leaf salves are used for dry and irritated skin.

Antioxidant (Some research)

Violets contain rutin and other antioxidant flavonoids.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Not commonly sold as capsules — use the leaves and flowers as tea.

The herb by itself: 1–2 g (Dried leaf and flower steeped as tea (per cup)), Up to 3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Candied flowers to decorate cakes
  • A soothing syrup for dry coughs
  • Leaf salves for dry, irritated skin

How to prepare

Add fresh flowers and young leaves to salads. Pour hot water over flowers for a blue tea that turns pink with lemon.

Safety & cautions

Not related to the houseplant African violet. Roots and seeds can cause vomiting; use only flowers and leaves.
