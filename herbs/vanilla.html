Vanilla

Vanilla planifolia · Orchidaceae (orchid family) · Culinary, Calm & Mood

Benefits at a glance

Vanilla is a comforting spice from the seed pod of an orchid. Its warm aroma is widely considered calming and relaxing. Its vanillin has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in laboratory studies. Vanilla makes foods taste sweeter, helping you use less added sugar. It turns simple, healthy foods like oatmeal and yogurt into treats.

Overview

Vanilla comes from the cured seed pods of an orchid native to Mexico. Each flower opens for only one day and is usually pollinated by hand. Vanilla's warm aroma is widely considered comforting and relaxing.

What it does in your body

  • Nervous system: Comforting aroma.
  • Whole body: Antioxidant.
  • Taste: Enhances sweetness so you can use less sugar.

How it works chemically

  • Vanillin (Phenolic aldehyde): The main flavor; antioxidant and anti-inflammatory in lab studies.
  • p-Hydroxybenzaldehyde (Phenolic aldehyde): Supporting flavor compound.
  • Vanillic acid (Phenolic acid): Antioxidant.

Benefits explained

Comforting aroma (Traditional use)

Vanilla's scent is widely considered calming, and it's often used in relaxation products.

Antioxidant (Some research)

Vanillin has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in laboratory studies.

Using less sugar (Traditional use)

Vanilla's aroma makes foods taste sweeter, helping you cut back on added sugar.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Not taken as capsules — use in food.

The herb by itself: 1–2 teaspoons (5–10 ml) pure extract. Pure vanilla extract in food.

Traditional uses

  • Flavoring baking, custards, drinks and oatmeal
  • A comforting, relaxing aroma
  • Homemade vanilla extract as a gift

How to prepare

Split a pod lengthwise and scrape out the tiny seeds. For extract, soak 5 split pods in a cup of vodka for 2 months.

Safety & cautions

Very safe in food. Imitation vanilla (vanillin) is not the same as real vanilla.
