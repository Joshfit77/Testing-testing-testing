Tarragon

Artemisia dracunculus · Asteraceae (daisy family) · Culinary, Digestion

Benefits at a glance

Tarragon is a classic French herb that stimulates appetite and digestion. It encourages the release of digestive juices before meals. A small study found tarragon extract improved insulin sensitivity. It contains eugenol, which was traditionally used to numb toothaches. It adds bright anise flavor to chicken, eggs and fish.

Overview

Tarragon's species name means 'little dragon,' perhaps from its coiled roots. It's one of the French fines herbes and the defining flavor of béarnaise sauce. Traditionally, it was chewed to numb toothaches and used to stimulate appetite.

What it does in your body

  • Digestive system: Stimulates appetite and digestion.
  • Metabolism: May improve insulin sensitivity.
  • Mouth: Mild numbing effect.

How it works chemically

  • Estragole (methyl chavicol) (Phenylpropanoid): Main flavor compound; safe in food, but avoid concentrated amounts long-term.
  • Eugenol (Phenylpropanoid): Numbing and anti-inflammatory.
  • Herniarin & other coumarins (Coumarins): Mildly anti-inflammatory.

Benefits explained

Appetite and digestion (Traditional use)

Tarragon stimulates digestive juices and has long been used to encourage appetite.

Blood sugar (Some research)

A small study found a tarragon extract improved insulin sensitivity.

Toothache numbing (Traditional use)

Tarragon contains eugenol, the numbing compound in cloves, and was once chewed for toothache.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Not commonly sold as capsules — use in cooking.

The herb by itself: 0.5–2 g (Fresh or dried leaf in food), With meals.

Traditional uses

  • Flavoring chicken, eggs, fish and béarnaise sauce
  • Stimulating appetite and digestion
  • Infusing vinegar for salad dressings

How to prepare

Use fresh leaves sparingly at the end of cooking; the flavor is strong.

Safety & cautions

Safe in food. Contains estragole, so avoid large medicinal amounts over long periods and during pregnancy.
