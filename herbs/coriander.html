Coriander & Cilantro

Coriandrum sativum · Apiaceae (carrot family) · Culinary, Digestion

Benefits at a glance

Coriander gives two foods in one: fresh cilantro leaves and warm coriander seeds. The seeds relax the gut and ease gas, bloating and IBS discomfort. Early research suggests coriander may help balance blood sugar. The fresh leaves supply vitamins A and K and antioxidants such as quercetin. It is a flavorful way to support digestion every day.

Overview

The leaves (cilantro) and the dried seeds (coriander) come from the same plant. Coriander seeds were found in Tutankhamun's tomb. Some people taste cilantro as soapy because of a genetic variation in smell receptors.

What it does in your body

  • Digestive system: Eases gas, cramping and IBS discomfort.
  • Metabolism: May help regulate blood sugar.
  • Whole body: Antioxidant vitamins in the leaves.

How it works chemically

  • Linalool (Monoterpene alcohol): Main oil of the seed; relaxes intestinal muscle and calms.
  • Petroselinic acid (Fatty acid): Anti-inflammatory fatty acid in the seed oil.
  • Quercetin (Flavonoid): Antioxidant in the fresh leaves.

Benefits explained

Digestive relief (Some research)

Coriander seed relaxes the digestive tract. In one clinical trial, a remedy containing coriander reduced pain and bloating in irritable bowel syndrome.

Blood sugar balance (Some research)

Animal studies show coriander seed may help lower blood sugar; human studies are still limited.

Antioxidants and vitamins (Traditional use)

Fresh cilantro is rich in vitamin K, vitamin A and antioxidants such as quercetin.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 500–1000 mg (Powdered coriander seed), 2 times a day.

The herb by itself: 1 g (Crushed seed steeped as tea (per cup)), Up to 3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Coriander seed tea to relieve gas and bloating
  • Fresh leaves to finish salsas, curries, noodle soups and tacos
  • Toasted ground seed in spice blends and baking

How to prepare

Add fresh leaves at the very end of cooking. Lightly toast seeds in a dry pan, then crush them for tea or seasoning.

Safety & cautions

Generally safe. Rarely causes allergy, especially in people allergic to fennel, celery or carrot.
