Slippery Elm

Ulmus rubra · Ulmaceae (elm family) · Digestion, Breathing & Throat

Benefits at a glance

Slippery elm is a soothing inner bark used in North American folk medicine. Its mucilage coats and calms sore throats and is used in throat lozenges. It comforts the digestive tract and may ease heartburn and irritable bowels. It was traditionally applied as a paste to soothe wounds and burns. Mixed into warm water or oatmeal, it makes a gentle, nourishing gruel.

Overview

Native American peoples used slippery elm inner bark for wounds, sore throats and as a food during times of scarcity. Like marshmallow, it is rich in soothing mucilage and is found today in many throat lozenges.

What it does in your body

  • Throat: Soothes sore throats.
  • Digestive system: Coats and calms the gut lining.
  • Skin: Soothing poultice.

How it works chemically

  • Mucilage (Polysaccharides (hexoses, pentoses)): Forms a slippery gel that coats irritated tissue and may trigger the gut to produce protective mucus.
  • Tannins (Polyphenols): Mild astringent.
  • Phytosterols (Plant sterols): Mild anti-inflammatory.

Benefits explained

Sore throats (Traditional use)

Mucilage in the inner bark coats and soothes the throat. It is an approved soothing ingredient in throat lozenges in the U.S.

Digestive comfort (Some research)

Traditionally used for heartburn and irritable bowels. A small study found a formula containing slippery elm improved bowel habits and bloating.

Skin poultice (Traditional use)

Native American and early American healers used slippery elm paste on wounds, boils and burns.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 800–1500 mg (Powdered slippery elm bark (with a full glass of water)), 3 times a day.

The herb by itself: 1.5–3 g (Powder stirred into warm water or oatmeal), Up to 3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Lozenges or tea for sore throats and coughs
  • A gruel to comfort an irritated digestive tract
  • A soothing paste for minor skin irritation

How to prepare

Whisk 1 teaspoon of powdered bark into warm water or oatmeal to make a smooth gruel.

Safety & cautions

May slow absorption of other medicines; take 1–2 hours apart. Traditionally, the outer bark was believed to cause miscarriage — avoid during pregnancy.
