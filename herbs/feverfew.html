Feverfew

Tanacetum parthenium · Asteraceae (daisy family) · Aches & Joints

Benefits at a glance

Feverfew is a small daisy traditionally used to prevent migraines. Its parthenolide reduces inflammation and the release of serotonin from platelets. A major review found it may slightly reduce how often migraines occur. It was traditionally used to bring down fevers. It must be taken daily for several weeks to see benefits.

Overview

Its name comes from the Latin febrifugia, 'fever reducer.' Feverfew became popular in the 1970s for migraines after reports from people who ate the leaves daily. Some studies suggest it may reduce how often migraines occur when taken regularly.

What it does in your body

  • Brain & blood vessels: Helps prevent migraines.
  • Whole body: Anti-inflammatory.
  • Immune system: Traditionally reduces fevers.

How it works chemically

  • Parthenolide (Sesquiterpene lactone): Blocks NF-κB, reduces release of serotonin from platelets, and lowers inflammatory prostaglandins — processes involved in migraine.
  • Tanetin (Flavonoid): Anti-inflammatory.
  • Camphor (Monoterpene ketone): Aromatic.

Benefits explained

Migraine prevention (Some research)

Parthenolide may reduce blood vessel inflammation and the release of serotonin from platelets. A Cochrane review found feverfew may slightly reduce how often migraines occur.

Fevers (Traditional use)

As its name suggests, feverfew was traditionally used to reduce fevers.

Anti-inflammatory (Some research)

Parthenolide reduces inflammation in laboratory studies.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 50–150 mg (Feverfew leaf (0.2–0.4% parthenolide)), Once a day, for at least 4 weeks.

The herb by itself: Capsules preferred. Chewing fresh leaves can cause mouth sores — use the capsule.

Traditional uses

  • Traditional support in preventing migraines
  • Easing tension headaches
  • A cheerful, long-flowering garden plant

How to prepare

Use standardized capsules daily; effects can take 4–6 weeks.

Safety & cautions

Avoid during pregnancy and with daisy-family allergies. Chewing fresh leaves may cause mouth sores. It may increase bleeding with blood thinners. Stopping suddenly after long use can cause rebound headaches.
