Dandelion

Taraxacum officinale · Asteraceae (daisy family) · Digestion, Culinary

Benefits at a glance

Dandelion is a humble plant that supports the liver and digestion. Its bitter compounds stimulate bile and digestive juices, helping the body digest fats. The leaves act as a natural diuretic and are rich in potassium. The root contains inulin, a prebiotic fiber that feeds healthy gut bacteria. The leaves are also a nutritious green full of vitamins A, C and K.

Overview

Every part of the dandelion is edible. Its leaves are rich in vitamins A, C and K and potassium, and its roasted root makes a coffee-like drink. Bitter herbs like dandelion are traditionally used to stimulate appetite and digestion.

What it does in your body

  • Liver & gallbladder: Stimulates bile flow.
  • Kidneys: Increases urine (leaf); rich in potassium.
  • Gut: Feeds beneficial bacteria (root inulin).

How it works chemically

  • Taraxacin & sesquiterpene lactones (Bitter compounds): Trigger bitter taste receptors, which signal the stomach, liver and gallbladder to release digestive juices and bile.
  • Inulin (Prebiotic fiber): Fermented by gut bacteria into short-chain fatty acids that nourish the gut lining.
  • Potassium (Mineral): Replaces the potassium lost through the diuretic effect.

Benefits explained

Supporting the liver and digestion (Traditional use)

Dandelion's bitter compounds stimulate bile flow and digestive juices. This is why it has been a classic 'liver herb' and spring tonic for centuries.

Natural diuretic (Some research)

A small human study found dandelion leaf extract increased how often people urinated, supporting its traditional use for water retention. The leaves are naturally high in potassium.

Feeding good gut bacteria (Some research)

Dandelion root is rich in inulin, a prebiotic fiber that feeds beneficial gut bacteria.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 500–1000 mg (Powdered dandelion root), 2–3 times a day.

The herb by itself: 3–4 g (Dried root simmered as tea (per cup)), 3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Bitter leaves before meals to wake up digestion
  • Roasted root as a caffeine-free coffee alternative
  • Leaf tea as a traditional gentle diuretic

How to prepare

Toss young leaves into salads or sauté with garlic. Roast chopped root until brown, grind and brew like coffee.

Safety & cautions

Avoid if allergic to ragweed or daisies. It may interact with diuretics, lithium and some antibiotics. Check with your doctor if you have gallbladder problems.
