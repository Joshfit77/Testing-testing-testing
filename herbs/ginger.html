Ginger

Zingiber officinale · Zingiberaceae (ginger family) · Digestion, Immune Support, Aches & Joints, Culinary

Benefits at a glance

Ginger is one of the most trusted herbs for nausea, including motion sickness and morning sickness. Its gingerols act on the gut and on serotonin receptors involved in feeling sick. Ginger also calms inflammation pathways and may modestly ease joint pain and muscle soreness after exercise. It helps the stomach empty more quickly, reducing bloating and heaviness after meals. Warming and versatile, it also brings comfort during colds when taken as tea with lemon and honey.

Overview

Ginger has been used in Asian and Indian traditions for over 5,000 years and was one of the first spices traded to Europe. It contains gingerols and shogaols that give its heat. Ginger is one of the best-researched herbs for nausea, including motion sickness and morning sickness.

What it does in your body

  • Digestive system: Settles nausea and helps the stomach empty.
  • Musculoskeletal system: Eases joint and muscle pain.
  • Circulation: Warms and slightly thins the blood.

How it works chemically

  • Gingerols (Phenolic ketones): Block serotonin 5-HT3 receptors in the gut (the same target as anti-nausea medicines) and inhibit COX and LOX inflammation enzymes.
  • Shogaols (Phenolic ketones (form on drying)): Stronger anti-inflammatory and antioxidant versions of gingerols.
  • Zingerone (Phenolic compound): Formed on cooking; antioxidant and soothing to the gut.

Benefits explained

Nausea relief (Well researched)

Gingerols and shogaols act on the stomach and on serotonin receptors involved in feeling sick. Clinical trials support ginger for pregnancy-related nausea and motion sickness, and many pregnancy guidelines list it as a first option to try.

Joint and muscle comfort (Some research)

Ginger blocks some of the same inflammation pathways (COX enzymes) as common pain relievers. Studies suggest modest reductions in osteoarthritis pain and in muscle soreness after exercise.

Healthy digestion (Some research)

Ginger helps the stomach empty more quickly, which can reduce heaviness, bloating and indigestion after meals.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 250–500 mg (Powdered ginger root), 2–4 times a day (max 4,000 mg a day).

The herb by itself: 0.5–1 g (Dried ground ginger in water, food or tea), 2–3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Settling nausea from travel or an upset stomach
  • A warming drink with lemon and honey during cold season
  • Supporting comfort in stiff joints and sore muscles

How to prepare

Simmer 4–6 thin slices of fresh root in 2 cups of water for 10 minutes. Grate fresh ginger into stir-fries, soups, smoothies and marinades.

Safety & cautions

Large amounts may increase bleeding risk with blood thinners and can cause heartburn. Speak to your doctor if you have gallstones or are pregnant before using more than food amounts.
