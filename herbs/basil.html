Sweet Basil

Ocimum basilicum · Lamiaceae (mint family) · Culinary, Digestion

Benefits at a glance

Sweet basil brings flavor and gentle wellness to the kitchen. Its oils relax the digestive tract and ease gas and bloating. Basil contains eugenol, which calms inflammation through the same enzymes targeted by some pain relievers. Its flavonoids help protect cells from oxidative damage. Fresh leaves also add vitamin K, vitamin A and minerals to meals.

Overview

Basil likely originated in India and traveled to the Mediterranean along spice routes. Its name comes from the Greek basileus, 'king.' Fresh basil contains vitamin K and aromatic oils such as linalool and eugenol that give its sweet-spicy scent.

What it does in your body

  • Digestive system: Relieves gas and bloating.
  • Whole body: Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant.
  • Blood: Provides vitamin K for clotting.

How it works chemically

  • Eugenol (Phenylpropanoid): Inhibits COX enzymes that make inflammatory prostaglandins.
  • Linalool (Monoterpene alcohol): Relaxes the gut and calms.
  • Orientin & vicenin (Flavonoids): Protect cells and DNA from oxidative damage.

Benefits explained

Digestive comfort (Traditional use)

Basil's eugenol and linalool relax the gut, and it is traditionally taken after meals as a tea to ease gas and bloating.

Anti-inflammatory compounds (Some research)

Eugenol in basil blocks COX enzymes in laboratory studies — the same target as some pain relievers — and its flavonoids help protect cells from damage.

Nutrient boost (Traditional use)

A generous handful of fresh basil adds vitamin K, vitamin A and manganese to everyday meals.

How much to take (adults 100 lb and over)

Capsule form: 500 mg (Powdered basil leaf), 1–2 times a day.

The herb by itself: 1–2 g (Dried leaf steeped as tea (per cup)), 2–3 times a day.

Traditional uses

  • Fresh leaves to brighten tomato dishes, salads and pasta
  • A mild after-meal tea to ease gas and bloating
  • Infused water with strawberries or lemon

How to prepare

Tear rather than chop leaves to prevent bruising, and add at the end of cooking. Blend with olive oil, garlic, nuts and cheese for pesto.

Safety & cautions

Safe in food. Its vitamin K may matter for people taking warfarin if eaten in large amounts — keep intake consistent.
